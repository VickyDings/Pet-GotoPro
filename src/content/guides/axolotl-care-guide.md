Axolotls get shelved next to tropical fish, and almost everything that applies to tropical fish is wrong for them. They need cold water, not heated. Still water, not filtered hard. And the medications sitting on the same shop shelf can kill them.

Here is what actually matters, in the order you need it.

## Temperature: the thing that actually kills them

Axolotls come from cool highland water. Sustained warmth suppresses the immune system and appetite, and fungal infection follows within days. **There is no scenario in which an axolotl tank needs a heater** — if a shop sells you a starter kit with one in it, take it out.

| Temperature | Status | What you'll see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 60–68°F / 16–20°C | **Ideal** | Good appetite, full forward gills, active at dusk |
| 68–72°F / 20–22°C | Upper limit | Survivable short-term. Start cooling now. |
| 72–76°F / 22–24°C | Stress zone | Appetite drops, gills shrink, floating, heavy slime |
| Above 76°F / 24°C | Emergency | Fungal and bacterial infection, death within days |

Cooling options, cheapest first: put the tank in the coolest room; lift the lid and run a clip-on fan across the surface for 2–4°F; air-condition the room; or fit an aquarium chiller, which is the only method that holds an exact temperature through a heatwave.

## Cycle the tank before you buy the animal

Axolotl skin is permeable, so water chemistry passes straight into them. In a mature tank, bacteria convert toxic ammonia to nitrite and then to much less toxic nitrate, which you export with weekly water changes. Growing those colonies takes **4–8 weeks**, and it must happen before the animal arrives.

Dose ammonia to 2–4 ppm and wait until a 2 ppm dose reads **0 ammonia and 0 nitrite 24 hours later**. Bringing an axolotl home to an uncycled tank is the most common beginner mistake, and it shows up as chemical burns on the gills within two weeks.

Targets once running: ammonia 0, nitrite 0, nitrate under 20 ppm, pH 7.4–7.6, GH 7–14 dGH, KH 3–8 dKH. Use a liquid test kit — paper strips are not accurate enough for ammonia and nitrite, the two readings that actually matter.

## Tank and substrate

A single adult needs a **20-gallon long as a minimum**; a 29 or 40-gallon breeder is meaningfully better. What matters is floor area, because axolotls spend almost all their time walking on the bottom.

Axolotls feed by suction — they lunge and vacuum in whatever is nearby. That reflex makes substrate a life-or-death choice:

- **Bare glass** — safest, zero impaction risk
- **Fine aquarium sand under 1 mm** — recommended for adults over about 5 inches
- **Gravel, pebbles, glass beads** — never. Swallowed during feeding strikes, causing blockage and death.

Keep flow gentle. Axolotls are ambush predators from still canal water, and constant current exhausts them and curls their gills permanently forward. A sponge filter is close to ideal. Add two hides per animal, low-light plants like Anubias and Java fern, dim lighting, and a secure lid.

**No tankmates.** Fish nip gills and carry parasites, small animals get inhaled, and other axolotls bite each other's limbs.

## Diet

Obligate carnivores, and one food covers almost everything: **earthworms — Canadian or European nightcrawlers**. High protein, cheap, near-complete nutrition, almost universally accepted. A quality soft sinking pellet is the backup.

Adults over 9 inches eat every 2–3 days; juveniles daily; hatchlings twice daily on live food.

**Never feed:** feeder fish (parasites, and many contain thiaminase), mealworms or crickets (chitin causes impaction), snails and shelled shrimp, beef heart or raw meat, and anything wild-caught.

Unlike reptiles, axolotls need **no calcium dusting, no D3 and no UVB**. The supplements that matter go into the water.

## Reading your axolotl

Gills and tail are the dashboard. **Gills curled sharply forward** or a **tail tip hooked to one side** mean stress from flow, heat or ammonia — early warnings that reverse once the cause is gone. Red inflamed gill stalks mean ammonia burn. White cottony tufts mean fungus. Refusing food for several days means check temperature, then water, then impaction, in that order.

Three treatments worth knowing before you need them: **tubbing** (a clean tub with 100% daily water changes — the universal first response), **salt baths** (2–3 tsp non-iodised salt per litre, 10–15 minutes, supervised), and **fridging** (41–46°F for impaction, under veterinary guidance).

Find an exotics vet before you need one, and quarantine any new arrival for 30 days.
