Bearded dragons get sold as an easy first reptile, and in one sense that is fair — they are diurnal, they tolerate handling better than almost any other lizard, and their day-to-day care is genuinely simple. What is not simple is the equipment. They come from arid inland Australia, where the sun is ferocious, and reproducing that indoors costs real money and needs kit most starter bundles do not include.

Almost every problem we see comes down to that gap. Here is what actually matters, in the order you need it.

## Heat: they digest with it, not despite it

Bearded dragons are ectotherms, which means external heat is not a comfort — it is how their digestion runs. A dragon sitting below about 95°F cannot process the food in its gut, and undigested food is where impaction starts.

The numbers, measured at the **surface** rather than in the air:

- **Basking surface:** 100–105°F (38–41°C) for adults, 105–110°F (41–43°C) under a year
- **Warm end, air:** 88–92°F (31–33°C)
- **Cool end, air:** 75–85°F (24–29°C)
- **Night:** 65–75°F (18–24°C), all lights off — a drop is healthy
- **Humidity:** 30–40%

Point an infrared temp gun at the basking platform itself; use digital probes for the air at each end. The stick-on dial thermometers that come in starter kits are routinely wrong by 10–20 degrees and only read the glass they are stuck to.

Never use a heat rock — they cause contact burns and there is no safe version. And a heat mat is not a primary heat source: dragons absorb heat through their backs and do not reliably sense belly heat, which is exactly how mat burns happen.

## UVB: the part most people get wrong

UVB drives vitamin D3 synthesis, which drives calcium absorption. Without it a dragon can eat all the calcium in the world and still develop metabolic bone disease — soft jaw, bowed limbs, tremors, fractures. Advanced MBD is permanent.

There is no supplement that fully replaces good UVB, and no way to eyeball whether it is working. Get the specification right:

- A **linear T5 HO** tube, 10.0 / 12% / desert grade
- Running about **two thirds** of the enclosure length, at the warm end
- Mounted **inside** the enclosure, in a reflector — a mesh screen absorbs roughly a third of the output
- Aiming for a **UV index of 4.0–6.0** at the basking spot
- On a timer for **12–14 hours** a day
- **Replaced every 12 months**

Compact coil bulbs are not a substitute at any price. They produce very little usable UVB over a very small area, and they are the most common cause of MBD in pet bearded dragons.

## Substrate: what they can and cannot swallow

Slate or ceramic tile is the best all-round answer. It cannot be swallowed, it holds basking heat so the whole surface radiates warmth, it wipes clean in seconds, and it files their nails as they walk. Most tile shops will cut it to your enclosure dimensions free.

A properly built bioactive soil mix — roughly 60% topsoil, 30% play sand, 10% clay, with springtails and isopods — is excellent enrichment and lets them dig naturally. It is more work to establish and needs correct temperatures to stay dry.

What to avoid entirely: **calci-sand** and **crushed walnut shell**, both sold as reptile substrate and both well known for causing fatal impaction, and **loop-pile reptile carpet**, whose loops catch claws and cost toes.

## Diet: the ratio that flips

This is the most-missed fact in the hobby. A bearded dragon's diet **inverts as it grows**.

| Age | Insects : plants | Frequency |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–3 months | ~80 : 20 | Insects 3× daily |
| 3–12 months | ~70 : 30 | Insects 2× daily |
| 12–18 months | ~50 : 50 | Insects once daily, tapering |
| 18 months+ | ~20 : 80 | Insects 2–4× weekly, salad daily |

An adult still eating a juvenile's insect load becomes obese and develops fatty liver disease. By eighteen months the salad is the main meal.

**Best staple feeders:** dubia roaches, and black soldier fly larvae (calciworms), which have a naturally favourable calcium-to-phosphorus ratio. Crickets are fine but noisy and they escape. Hornworms are around 85% water — useful for a dehydrated or constipated dragon, too low in protein to be a staple.

**Never feed:** fireflies or any glowing insect, which contain cardiac toxins and can kill a bearded dragon outright; avocado; rhubarb; onion and garlic; and any insect caught in the garden.

**Greens for the salad:** collard, mustard, turnip and dandelion greens, escarole, endive and watercress as staples, with squash and bell pepper mixed in. Skip spinach and beet greens — their oxalates bind the calcium you are working to provide.

**Supplements:** feeder insects are naturally low in calcium and high in phosphorus, which is backwards for a lizard. Dust with plain phosphorus-free calcium (no D3) on most insect feedings, add calcium with D3 once or twice weekly for adults, and a multivitamin weekly. Both D3 and vitamin A are fat-soluble and accumulate, so follow a schedule rather than dusting everything with everything.

## Reading your dragon

They communicate visibly, which is half the fun of keeping one.

**Normal:** gaping on the basking spot (that is thermoregulation), a slow circular arm wave (a submission signal), head bobbing (a dominance display), brief beard darkening, and a winter slowdown known as brumation.

**Not normal:** a swollen or rubbery lower jaw, bowed legs or tremors, a beard that stays black for hours away from any threat, no stool for over a week, open-mouth breathing away from the heat, mucus at the nose, or yellow crusty patches under the scales.

Find an exotics vet before you need one, and take a fresh stool sample in during your first month — parasites are extremely common in shop-bought dragons, cheap to treat early and expensive to ignore.
